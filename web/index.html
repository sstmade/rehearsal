A flight simulator for robot AI

Before a home robot touches your things, let it rehearse.

Pilots train in simulators. A robot's first touch of your kitchen shouldn't be its first try. Rehearsal turns one photo of a table into a physics copy of it, where the robot's mistakes cost nothing and it can learn from them before it moves for real.

Act two · what we found

Three things physics told us about today's AI.

5 of 5

Frontier AI fails first try in everyday clutter.

On all five tables built to be a little awkward (a lamp, a kettle, a far tray), the model's first plan failed in physics every time.

17 of 17

Telling it “that failed” barely helps. Showing it what physics saw fixes it.

Given the simulator's log of what touched what, the model fixed its plan in 17 of 17 runs. With no feedback it managed 7 of 17; told only which goals had failed, 8 of 17. On the kitchen table: 5 of 5 fixed versus 0 of 5.

2 of 20

Passing once can be luck.

On the real photo, the plan passed the simulation, then worked only 2 of 20 times under small random nudges. The model chose to take a closer look at the cracker box, and went from 7 of 40 to 21 of 40 on fresh test runs.

Numbers from the project's evaluation (EVAL.md in the repository). All runs use Meta Muse Spark; physics is MuJoCo.

          5

          A closer look at one object

          If the plan is shaky, which single object is worth a second photo? Rehearsal tries re-measuring each one and reports the one that helps most.

          Technical details value of re-observing each object, selection vs held-out runs

          Extra successes if only this object is re-observed

          A second look is modelled as a smaller position uncertainty (σ 1.2 cm → 0.4 cm) for that object. The object is chosen on one set of random runs (“selection”) and the gain is then measured on a fresh set (“held-out”), so the headline number isn't flattered by the choice.

          6

          What was checked, and what was only assumed

          Every claim on this page is labelled by how it was earned. This is what a person should see before a robot is handed a mug.

          Checked by physics

          • Can the arm reach each object from above?
          • What touched what, and when
          • Did the goals hold at the end: on, inside, stacked, upright, still, nothing else disturbed

          Estimated

          • Friction and weight, varied by ±40 % and ±30 %
          • Where each object really is, within about a centimetre
          • The success rate, from 40 nudged runs

          Assumed, not checked

          • Grasping: a suction cup that holds whenever it lands on the top face
          • Objects are simple shapes (boxes, cylinders, bowls) for physics
          • Simulation only. No real robot was moved.
